# PM networking and outreach

Help the user get into conversations with the people who hire product managers, before or instead of a cold application. Referrals and warm introductions convert far better than applying through a careers page, and many PM roles are filled before they're posted.

## Non-negotiables

- Never invent a connection, shared experience, mutual contact or compliment. If you don't know why this person, say so and ask the user.
- Only claim experience that's in the base profile (see the job-search-cv-tailor skill). The same honesty rules apply to a LinkedIn message as to a CV.
- Draft only. The user reviews and sends every message themselves.
- One clear ask per message. Never ask a stranger for a job in a first message.
- Respect a no or a non-reply. Follow up at most twice, then stop.

## Step 0: Inputs

Use the base profile, personas and target roles if they exist. If they don't, ask for:
- The target: PM level (APM, PM, Senior, Group, Head of Product), domain (B2B SaaS, fintech, platform, growth and so on), and region or remote.
- A shortlist of target companies, or permission to help build one.
- What the user wants from conversations right now: learning about a company, a referral, or a hiring manager's attention.

## Step 1: Map the network

Build a target list, one row per person:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Name and role | For example "Sam, Senior PM, Payments" |
| Company | Target company |
| Tie | Warm (knows the user), Weak (shared employer, school, community or event) or Cold |
| Why them | The real reason this person is relevant to the user |
| Ask | Learn, Referral or Hiring conversation |
| Status | To contact, Sent, Replied, Chat booked, Referred, Closed |
| Next action and date | What happens next and when |

Prioritise in this order: warm ties at target companies, weak ties, then people one step from a warm tie (ask for an intro), then cold. For each target company, aim for two or three people across roles: a PM on the team, the hiring manager (often a Group PM or Head of Product), and a design or engineering peer who works with product. Recruiters are useful once a role is open, not before.

Suggest where to find weak ties: former colleagues now at the company, alumni groups, product communities (Mind the Product, ProductTank, Lenny's community, Women in Product, local meetups), and people who've written or spoken about a problem the user has worked on.

## Step 2: Write the message

Ask which type it is, then draft it. Keep LinkedIn connection notes under 300 characters and messages or emails under 120 words.

Every message has: why them (specific), who the user is in one line (tied to that person's work), one small ask, and an easy way out ("No worries if now's not a good time").

**Warm contact.** Casual and direct. Say what the user is looking for in one sentence, because people can only help if they know. Ask if they know anyone on the product team at the target company.

**Intro request.** Ask the warm contact for an intro and give them a short, forwardable blurb they can paste without editing.

**Cold to a PM.** Reference something real: a launch, a talk, a post, the product itself. Ask a specific question or for 15 to 20 minutes to learn how product works at the company. Don't mention a job.

**Cold to a hiring manager.** Only when a role is open or the team is clearly growing. Lead with the problem the team is solving and one proven result from the base profile that maps to it (reuse the role-mapping table if it exists). Ask whether they're open to a short conversation.

**Referral ask.** Only after a real conversation, or with a warm contact. Name the specific role and link, say why the user fits in two lines, and offer a short blurb and their CV so referring takes the contact two minutes.

**Thank-you.** Within 24 hours of a chat. Name one thing the user learned and anything they promised to send.

**Follow-up.** After about a week with no reply, one short nudge that adds something new (a relevant thought, an update or the open role). After a second silence, stop and mark it Closed.

Give one draft, then offer a shorter or warmer variant if the user wants it. Read each draft out loud in your head: it should sound like a person, not a template.

## Step 3: Prepare for the chat

For a coffee chat or informational call, give the user:
- A 30-second introduction: current role, the PM problem space they're drawn to, and why this company.
- Five or six questions that reveal how product works there, for example: How are roadmap decisions made, and who has the final call? How close are PMs to customers? What does a strong first six months look like for a PM here? What's the hardest product problem the team has right now? How do product, design and engineering split ownership?
- One closing ask, chosen beforehand: "Is there anyone else you'd suggest I talk to?" or, if the chat went well and a role is open, a referral.
- A note to listen more than talk. The goal is to learn and be remembered, not to pitch.

After the chat, ask for the user's notes and pull out: insights to use in a cover letter or interview, names to contact next, and any promised follow-up.

## Step 4: Keep it moving

- Suggest a steady weekly rhythm, for example five new outreaches and all follow-ups due, rather than bursts.
- When asked how networking is going, report reply rate (replies divided by messages sent), chat rate and referrals. Low replies point to targeting or the message; chats without referrals point to the ask or the fit. With fewer than about 15 messages, say the numbers are too small to read much into.
- When a conversation becomes a referral or an application, suggest logging it with the job-search-tracker skill and noting the referrer in the Referral notes column.
- Remind the user to give back: share a useful article, congratulate people on launches, and offer help to others in the search. Networking that only asks wears thin.
