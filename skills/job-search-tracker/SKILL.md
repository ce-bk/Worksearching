# Job search tracker, funnel insights and negotiation

Help the user run their job search like a funnel: track it, measure it, fix the leaks, and negotiate well at the end.

## 1. The tracking log

One row per application, with these columns:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Company | Company name |
| Position | Job title and level |
| Job link | Link to the posting |
| Referral notes | Who referred them, or the connection and research notes |
| CV version | Link or file name of the tailored CV |
| Cover letter | Link or file name |
| Stage | Research, Applied, Interview 1, Interview 2, Final, Offer, Signed, Rejected or Withdrawn |
| Got interview | Yes or No (feeds the funnel) |
| Interview dates | Scheduled dates and follow-ups due |
| Post-interview notes | Feedback, learning, and what could have gone differently |
| Outcome | Final result and reason if known |

Store the log wherever the user keeps it: a spreadsheet, a Notion table or a Google Sheet. If they share a file, read it and return the updated rows. If there's no file, give the log as a markdown table they can paste.

When the user reports an interview, ask for the post-interview notes. Push for "What could I have done differently?" as well as "What went well?"

## 2. Funnel analysis

When the user asks how the search is going:

- Interview rate = interviews divided by applications. Cold applications often convert at low single digits. Referrals and targeted applications convert much higher. Present these as rough reference points, not hard benchmarks.
- Offer rate = offers divided by interviews. Also track the final-round rate.
- Locate the leak:
  - Few interviews: a targeting or CV problem.
  - Interviews but no final rounds: an interview performance problem.
  - Finals but no offers: a fit, negotiation or stakeholder problem.
- Group rejection reasons (missing skill, timing, seniority mismatch, chose someone else, no reason given).
- Give three specific recommendations, each tied to a number in the log.
- With fewer than about 10 applications, say the numbers are too small to read much into.

If the interview rate is strong, focus on conversion. Most searches leak between interview and offer.

## 3. Sourcing and networking

- Tell your network specifically what you're looking for. Many roles are shared before they're posted, and referrals convert far better than cold applications.
- Prefer fewer, better-targeted applications over volume. Check each role against the persona before applying.
- Use LinkedIn alongside niche job boards. Ask the user which boards serve their sector or region.

## 4. Salary and offer negotiation

Before the conversation:
- Research market rates from several sources (Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, peers, and community input).
- Define the BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement): the realistic option if this offer falls through.
- Decide what they actually want: base, equity, title, flexibility, learning budget.

During the conversation:
- Avoid naming a number first if you can. Deflect with: "I'd like to understand the role and how compensation is structured first."
- Clarify scope: people management or individual contributor, and the split between strategy and execution.
- Ask for the typical range for the level.
- Compare total compensation (base, equity, bonus, benefits, flexibility), not base alone. A lower base with strong equity can be the better deal.
- Treat a missing range or a vague role as a red flag.

After the conversation: help the user draft a short, specific counter-offer, and suggest they rehearse it out loud. Pay norms and contract rules vary by country, so flag anything jurisdiction-specific and recommend checking local norms or a professional adviser for the detail.

## 5. Weekly check-in (optional)

If the user wants a regular rhythm, ask four questions: applications sent this week, interviews and outcomes, follow-ups due, and one thing to change next week.
